Speaking script

Once the recordings are back in time, the next question is whether we can recover operationally meaningful parts of the incident from what people are saying.

For this pilot I ran raw Whisper locally as a baseline. Whisper itself only transcribes. A separate script searches those transcripts using operational phrases that came from our FDNY discussions and Steve's follow-up emails. That gives us candidate moments to review and place back into the incident.

Some examples are fairly clear when I listen back, like “heavy fire,” “making a push,” and “10-45.” Those are useful because they start to look like incident milestones rather than isolated words. But a phrase can still be mistranscribed, misheard, retrospective, or meaningless without surrounding context. That is exactly why the next example is more important than the clean ones.

Validation / next steps

Validation requirements before scaling.

One fire is enough for a technical pilot. It is not enough to validate the research claim. We need expert interpretation and a correct model of the recording system before moving from one incident to many.

Expert interpretation

  • What do difficult transmissions actually say?
  • How much surrounding context is normally needed?
  • Which operational signals are both useful and realistically detectable?
  • Do expert-corrected audio / transcript pairs already exist?
  • If not, could a small subset be professionally reviewed?

Recording system & data

  • What creates each recording file and timestamp?
  • What does temporal overlap mean?
  • What happens when another member keys up during existing traffic?
  • How do chief-rig / receiver recordings differ?
  • What metadata and larger internal archive can we access?

Speaking script

This one incident is enough to test the technical pipeline, but it is not enough to make a general claim about FDNY operations.

The next step depends on two things. First, whether people with actual fireground expertise agree that we are extracting and interpreting useful information. Second, whether we understand how these recordings are created well enough to know what the timeline actually represents.

If we can validate those pieces, then the interesting long-term question becomes whether incident-level reconstruction can help search a much larger archive and surface incidents or periods that deserve human review. That is where expert-corrected recordings, the richer internal archive, receiver metadata, and the Chief of Safety pathway become important.

Additional reference details (for questions)
Earlier FDNY discussion described approximately 15 years of internal recordings at roughly 100 incidents per day and explained that multiple chief rigs can capture the same operation differently because of receiver position and line-of-sight. Treat those archive-size numbers as stakeholder-reported context, not a verified inventory.
