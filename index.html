Speaking script

The literature is broader than fire specifically. We found work on firefighter radio, police radio, emergency-response support, and traffic incident detection.

Our selected literature includes firefighter-radio automatic speech recognition, or ASR, research from 2012. Earlier approaches adapted acoustic and language models specifically to the radio domain. More recent work uses pretrained speech-to-text models, domain fine-tuning, and large language models for extracting or summarizing information after transcription.

Across these applications, the recurring problem is that radio speech is unusually difficult: it’s short, noisy, coded, and full of domain-specific terms. One thing that became clear to us is that transcription and useful incident analysis are two separate technical problems.

Additional reference details (for questions)
The 2012 study describes the PRONTO corpus of German firefighter exercises. CLEAR-Command uses Whisper for transcription and GPT-4, an existing large language model, for summarization and task extraction. The 2026 Purdue report is follow-up work related to the 2025 traffic paper, not an independent research line.

References: Stein & Usabaev, 2012; Srivastava et al., 2024; Doula et al., 2025; Gartner et al., 2025; Gartner et al., 2026.

Speaking script

The first paper focuses almost entirely on that first problem: transcription quality.

They worked with Chicago police radio. They downloaded roughly 80,000 hours of radio, but manually labeled 62,080 utterances, totaling 46.2 hours of transcribed speech.

The headline comparison is here. Whisper large-v3, an open-source speech-to-text model used off the shelf, had a test word error rate of 50.8 percent. A different speech-recognition model, FastConformer from NVIDIA’s NeMo toolkit, fine-tuned on police radio, scored 27.3 percent. This comparison changes both the model and its training conditions, so those two numbers alone do not isolate the effect of fine-tuning.

Word error rate counts substitutions, deletions, and insertions compared with a reference transcript. It does not mean that 27 percent of transmissions are wrong. The paper also shows substantial disagreement between human transcribers, which gives some sense of how difficult this audio is.

Our takeaway is that model choice and domain-specific training deserve evaluation on FDNY speech. We should test FDNY-specific adaptation rather than assume a generic model is sufficient.

Additional reference details (for questions)
Professional annotators had a median WER of 25.9% against other annotators; other annotators had 28.9%. Median means the middle value in the ordered set of scores. These human scores are not directly comparable to ASR test performance because annotation and evaluation conditions differ. Around 13% of utterances included an unintelligible segment. For FDNY evaluation, examine operationally important errors in units, codes, and numbers. The project implication is an inference.

Speaking script

The second paper looks at what you can do after you have a transcript.

They monitored 71 miles of I-65 across four county dispatch systems and collected more than 100,000 transmissions. Their pipeline is deliberately simple: scanner audio is recorded, Whisper Turbo converts it to text, keyword rules look for interstate and incident terms, and qualifying transmissions generate an automated email.

They flagged 87 transmissions and report a median recording-to-email latency of about 52 seconds. That is a measured pipeline latency; whether it is useful depends on the intended workflow.

There is an important limitation. They do not establish comprehensive ground truth for incident detection, so precision, recall, and missed broadcasts remain unquantified.

Their automated unit is essentially one transmission at a time. They do not demonstrate automatically connecting a sequence of transmissions into an evolving incident. That is a gap in the two studies we examined closely, and it is particularly interesting to us. We are not claiming that no prior research has addressed it.

Additional reference details (for questions)
The collection ran from May 12 to June 30, 2025, on rural I-65. Recording used ProScan. Location keywords could add mile-marker information. Figure 8 shows an actual output. The paper includes a small illustrative transcription/WER table. Of the 87 flagged transmissions, the authors report 37 automatically geolocated, 40 manually located, and 10 unlocated. Their text calls the located total 76, although 37 + 40 = 77; do not treat that inconsistent total as independently validated. This detail is for questions only, not the prepared talk.

Speaking script

That leads to our current idea.

We’re thinking of the project in three stages. First, transcribe the radio and evaluate how well speech recognition actually works for FDNY. Second, extract and connect operational information such as units, codes, locations, Maydays, and other events across multiple transmissions. Third, use that structure to surface candidate incidents for human review.

The working research focus is incident-level timeline reconstruction across multiple transmissions, but we don’t want to lock the downstream problem before understanding what is useful to FDNY.

One of the main things we want to discuss today is how radio communications are currently transcribed and used in after-action review, what information matters most, what kinds of incidents are difficult to find at scale, and which incident types or alarm classifications should be prioritized. We’d also like to understand whether alarm classification would help us sample incidents of different sizes.

Additional reference details (for questions)
We have not established that 10-codes or Maydays identify every near-miss, or that a flagged incident is a confirmed near-miss. Incident reconstruction is a proposed direction whose usefulness still needs FDNY input.

Speaking script

Finally, these are the resources we currently have.

Our current Broadcastify collection contains 176,576 call records, with 176,165 records containing transcripts, from a Brooklyn-focused collection between February and July 2026. These are individual call records, not 176,000 incidents.

For audio, Broadcastify is a potential historical source, although coverage is intermittent and we still need to establish exactly how much usable FDNY audio is available.

We also have an initial Whisper and faster-whisper pipeline and some exploratory work on code extraction, filtering, and audio indexing. Radiant Glow is a proprietary incident-analysis service whose free preview could let us compare outputs. Its methodology and validation are not published.

What would help us most from FDNY is domain expertise and, if available, reference material: existing audio archives, manual transcripts, the current transcription process, and incident or after-action records that could help us define and evaluate the task.

So that’s where we are today. We’d really like to use the rest of the meeting to understand what problem would be most useful from your perspective and how we should structure the research around the data and workflows you already have.
